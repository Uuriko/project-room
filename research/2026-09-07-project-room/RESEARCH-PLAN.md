## Question inventory (84)

### A. Product and demand
1. Who is the first user and who is the first payer?
2. Which recurring job is painful enough to warrant another tool?
3. Is Project Room a destination, a complement to existing tools, or both?
4. Is the product useful before a marketplace or a whole team joins?
5. Where will legitimate, funded work come from initially?
6. What makes contributors and requesters return voluntarily?
7. What should we explicitly refuse to build?

### B. Quiet, inclusive interaction
8. What should the first screen and first successful action be?
9. Which details must be visible before commitment, even in a minimal UI?
10. When should advanced features appear, and how do users find them again?
11. How should ordinary chat become an explicit task without accidental assignment?
12. What accessibility and mobile behavior cannot be sacrificed for visual simplicity?
13. Which events deserve interruption and which belong in a quiet catch-up?
14. How can people correct, undo, leave and recover without operator help?

### C. Participation, identity and authority
15. Is a shareable invitation enough for conversation, paid work and payouts alike?
16. Who is accountable for an agent and who receives its rewards?
17. What can an agent discover or volunteer for without a new user instruction?
18. How should human-only, AI-assisted and agent-permitted work differ?
19. How do we support newcomers without admitting unlimited low-quality work?
20. Which credentials and permissions should expire or be revocable?
21. How should delegated agents and subcontractors share scope and credit?

### D. Work lifecycle and coordination
22. What is the smallest common work record across all task types?
23. Should a task use applications, reservations or open submissions?
24. How do we prevent duplicate effort and handle abandoned reservations?
25. What happens when requirements change after somebody starts?
26. How are partial results, cancellation and changed deadlines handled fairly?
27. Which system is authoritative when work also exists in GitHub or elsewhere?
28. What can we truthfully track when someone only copies a prompt?

### E. Quality, review and evidence
29. What counts as success before anybody starts work?
30. When is human review necessary, and when are automatic checks sufficient?
31. What does independent review mean when agents share operators, models or sources?
32. Who reviews the reviewers and resolves disagreements?
33. How do we pay for review, diagnosis, coordination and useful failed attempts?
34. What evidence should be retained without collecting private thought traces?
35. How does feedback improve future work without creating a gameable score?

### F. Incentives and allocation
36. Fixed reward, hourly work, contest, recurring work or pooled allocation?
37. How should price, worker effort and uncertainty be communicated?
38. How do we avoid races, spam, collusion and rewarding volume over usefulness?
39. How should contributors find appropriate opportunities fairly?
40. Is bilateral work trade enough, or is a transferable credit system needed?
41. Which noncash resources actually help people and operator-controlled agents?
42. Can repeat relationships and paid trials reduce onboarding and review costs?

### G. Money and business operations
43. What do offered, reserved, funded, approved and paid actually mean?
44. Who is the contractual seller and who handles payment complaints?
45. How do payment timing, authorizations and long tasks fit together?
46. Who bears processing fees, refunds, chargebacks and negative balances?
47. Do tiny rewards remain economic after review and support costs?
48. How do team splits, reviewer fees, taxes and payout eligibility work?
49. What happens when payment fails after useful work is accepted?

### H. Governance, rights and privacy
50. Where and for which categories can the first paid pilot responsibly operate?
51. What worker-classification and platform-work obligations need professional review?
52. What rights in code, data and AI-assisted outputs are agreed before work starts?
53. What privacy, deletion and retention promises can the product actually keep?
54. Which tasks should be prohibited or require special approval?
55. What appeal, moderation and account-recovery process is proportionate?
56. How do work swaps, credits and cross-border expansion change obligations?

### I. Physical tasks and human welfare
57. Which real-world tasks are low-risk enough for a first trial?
58. What information can be public versus shared only with the assigned person?
59. What makes physical-task evidence credible without becoming surveillance?
60. What happens if conditions on site differ, become unsafe or require extra spending?
61. How do location, scheduling, expenses and failed visits affect compensation?
62. Who may perform the task, and when are licenses, insurance or age checks required?
63. How do agents coordinate physical work without impersonating humans or making unsupported claims?

### J. Agent interfaces and external contribution
64. Which canonical operations should the UI, API, MCP and skill expose?
65. What role, if any, should A2A or other interoperability protocols play?
66. How do external workers obtain only relevant context and submit versioned evidence?
67. How should clients retry, resume, cancel and reconcile uncertain outcomes?
68. What should discovery reveal publicly versus behind authorization?
69. How do we version contracts and keep documentation, prompts and clients consistent?
70. What would make an external agent integration genuinely easy and reliable?

### K. Compute, architecture and operations
71. How does Dasha Compute become a tool without becoming a second room backend?
72. What budgets, quotations, approvals and limits govern agent execution?
73. How do we reconcile provider timeouts, cancellation and possible duplicate spending?
74. What persistence, recovery, session and deployment work must precede expansion?
75. Where is the simple existing architecture sufficient, and what would justify splitting it?
76. Which costs, reliability failures and data-retention burdens need operational visibility?
77. How do we separate demo behavior, tested source, live behavior and provider guarantees?

### L. Evidence, sequencing and stop rules
78. What can simulated human journeys and actual agent tests establish, and what can they not?
79. What baseline should Project Room have to beat?
80. How do we compare one agent, several agents and human-agent collaboration fairly?
81. Which outcomes matter more than task/message/agent counts?

## Lane boundaries

- Root: inventory, current-project evidence, UI/accessibility, human-AI collaboration studies, reconciliation, critical-source spot checks, final planning/artifact QA.
- Research lane 1: demand, allocation, incentives, reciprocity and review economics.
- Research lane 2: identity/authority, protocol contracts, external contribution and Compute boundaries.
- Research lane 3: payment promises, labor/fairness, privacy/IP, jurisdiction and physical-task boundaries.

Workers return evidence, not complete reports or artifacts; they do not create further agents. The second wave begins only after first-wave evidence is merged. No web search can resolve founder preferences, legal clearance or actual demand; those become explicit decision/validation gates, not invented answers.

## Stop test

Every question must have a provisional disposition and a source-family link or an explicit non-researchable gap. Consequential claims need original support or a stated limitation. Stop additional searching when remaining uncertainty is empirical product validation, owner choice, professional advice or partner approval rather than discoverable public evidence.
